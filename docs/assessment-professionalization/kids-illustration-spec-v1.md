# InnerGeo Kids — Illustration Specification V1.0

**Status:** Professional Visual Standard  
**Scope:** Ages 6–8 and future 9–12 Kids assessments  
**Purpose:** Ensure illustrations support comprehension without creating measurement bias.

---

## 1. Core Principle

The illustration must help the child understand the activity.

It must not become the main reason the child chooses an answer.

The visual system should support:

Comprehension
→
Comparable Attractiveness
→
Neutral Representation
→
Fair Preference Measurement

---

## 2. Visual Bias Risk

Illustrations can unintentionally change the construct being measured.

A child may choose an activity because:

- the image uses brighter colours
- the character looks happier
- the background is more exciting
- the activity looks more expensive
- one option includes animals
- one option includes technology
- one character appears more successful
- one scene looks socially approved
- one activity contains a familiar brand

Therefore visual review is part of assessment review, not only graphic design.

---

## 3. Global Art Direction

Recommended style:

- simple editorial illustration
- friendly but not childish caricature
- clear action
- low visual clutter
- comparable detail level
- soft, balanced colour use
- neutral facial expressions
- consistent character scale
- consistent camera distance
- consistent background complexity

Avoid:

- hyper-realistic advertising imagery
- cinematic lighting
- dramatic action poses
- luxury aesthetics
- exaggerated facial emotion
- game-like reward effects
- collectible-card style rarity cues
- highly saturated hero illustrations

---

## 4. Composition Standard

Across scored images, maintain comparable:

- image size
- subject scale
- number of characters
- foreground complexity
- background complexity
- object count
- action intensity
- visual contrast
- lighting
- perspective

A scored option should not appear visually more important than another.

---

## 5. Colour Standard

Colour should support clarity, not preference manipulation.

Avoid situations where:

- Create is always more colourful
- Discover always uses glowing science effects
- Lead uses heroic or powerful colours
- Connect uses warm emotional colours
- Think uses cold “intelligent” colours
- Move uses dramatically brighter outdoor scenes

Domain identity should come from activity content, not colour coding alone.

---

## 6. Character Expression

Characters should generally appear:

- engaged
- attentive
- neutral-positive

Avoid:

- one option showing extreme happiness
- one option showing boredom
- one option showing frustration
- applause
- trophies
- admiration from others
- authority approval

The illustration must not tell the child which activity is socially rewarded.

---

## 7. Gender Representation

No domain should become visually associated with one gender.

Avoid patterns such as:

- boys always Build
- girls always Connect
- boys always Lead
- girls always Create
- boys always Think
- girls always Express

Representation should be mixed across the full item bank.

Do not force gender balance inside every single image when it would make the scene unnatural.

Balance should be evaluated across the bank.

---

## 8. Cultural Representation

Characters and environments should not imply that one culture is the default.

Across the bank include varied:

- skin tones
- hair textures
- clothing styles
- everyday environments

Avoid:

- national stereotypes
- ethnic costume as decoration
- religious symbolism unless required by the construct
- assumptions about family structure
- assumptions about wealth or housing type

Identity representation must not become the measured variable.

---

## 9. Socioeconomic Neutrality

Avoid unnecessary visual signals of wealth.

Do not make activities depend visually on:

- expensive laptops
- premium tablets
- robotics kits
- private laboratories
- luxury sports facilities
- professional studios
- designer materials
- private tutors
- large private bedrooms
- expensive musical instruments

Prefer ordinary accessible materials whenever possible.

---

## 10. Brand Neutrality

Do not use:

- LEGO
- Apple
- Nike
- Disney
- Marvel
- Minecraft
- Roblox
- branded sports equipment
- branded school products
- recognisable commercial mascots

Use generic objects and environments.

---

## 11. Create Domain

Illustrations may show:

- drawing
- shaping
- arranging
- designing
- imagining
- making rhythm
- creative transformation

Control risks:

- excessive colour
- polished finished artwork
- professional-looking art tools
- “beautiful” output becoming the reward

Show the process rather than artistic quality.

---

## 12. Discover Domain

Illustrations may show:

- observing
- testing
- comparing
- investigating
- looking for clues
- examining simple phenomena

Control risks:

- glowing laboratory effects
- science-coat stereotypes
- expensive microscopes
- visually spectacular experiments

Discovery should also be represented through everyday observation.

---

## 13. Build Domain

Illustrations may show:

- joining pieces
- arranging materials
- constructing
- repairing
- testing physical objects

Control risks:

- branded construction toys
- power tools
- professional workshops
- masculine stereotypes
- physical dexterity as the only participation method

Show multiple accessible ways of manipulating materials.

---

## 14. Think Domain

Illustrations may show:

- patterns
- sequencing
- classification
- strategy
- rule-based puzzles
- planning choices

Control risks:

- school examination imagery
- visible grades
- “smart child” stereotypes
- advanced mathematics
- speed competition

The goal is reasoning interest, not correctness or intelligence.

---

## 15. Connect Domain

Illustrations may show:

- explaining
- cooperating
- listening
- supporting
- practising together
- helping understanding

Control risks:

- one child appearing helpless
- “good child” moral framing
- adult praise
- emotional rescue scenes
- caregiving stereotypes

Show equal-status interpersonal interaction where possible.

---

## 16. Lead Domain

Illustrations may show:

- suggesting a plan
- organising roles
- initiating an activity
- coordinating choices
- presenting an idea

Control risks:

- boss posture
- pointing commands
- raised platform
- followers admiring leader
- trophies
- status symbols

Leadership should appear as coordination and initiative, not dominance.

---

## 17. Move Domain

Illustrations may show:

- outdoor exploration
- movement
- nature interaction
- environmental activity
- active participation

Control risks:

- athletic performance
- racing
- winning
- sports ability
- inaccessible physical requirements

Represent different valid forms of movement.

Do not make speed or strength the visual objective.

---

## 18. Express Domain

Illustrations may show:

- storytelling
- speaking
- acting
- explaining
- drawing sequences
- sharing messages

Control risks:

- stage-performance glamour
- microphones and applause
- extroversion stereotypes
- verbal speech as the only communication mode

Expression may include spoken, written, visual, signed, or performed communication.

---

## 19. Accessibility Standard

Illustrations should not imply that interest requires a single physical, sensory, or communication ability.

Review for:

- mobility diversity
- motor accessibility
- hearing diversity
- visual accessibility
- communication diversity

Not every image must explicitly depict disability.

The full bank should avoid systematically equating one body type or ability profile with one interest domain.

---

## 20. Age 6–8 Visual Load

For ages 6–8:

- one primary action per image
- minimal background narrative
- avoid complex symbolic interpretation
- avoid small text inside images
- avoid requiring reading to understand the illustration
- keep activity obvious within approximately 2–3 seconds

The image should clarify the item, not create another puzzle.

---

## 21. Image/Text Relationship

The image and wording must point to the same construct.

Reject an illustration if:

- text measures helping but image shows leadership
- text measures discovery but image mainly shows technology
- text measures creativity but image rewards artistic quality
- text measures movement but image primarily shows sport competition
- text measures expression but image primarily shows popularity

---

## 22. Pairwise Comparison Rule

If future items display multiple activity images together, each option must be reviewed for comparable:

- attractiveness
- action
- number of characters
- scale
- emotional tone
- colour
- background
- object novelty

Pairwise comparison is especially sensitive to visual bias.

---

## 23. Illustration Metadata

Each future production illustration should record:

- image ID
- item ID
- age band
- domain
- illustration version
- visual concept
- characters shown
- environment
- key objects
- accessibility notes
- bias-review status
- reviewer status
- approval status

---

## 24. Illustration Review Status

Suggested status model:

### Draft
Visual concept exists but has not been reviewed.

### Content Matched
Image accurately represents the intended item construct.

### Bias Reviewed
Attractiveness, identity, socioeconomic, accessibility, and stereotype risks reviewed.

### Approved
Ready for use in the candidate or production assessment.

---

## 25. Image Generation Standard

If AI-generated illustrations are used:

- use one controlled master visual style
- maintain consistent composition rules
- avoid prompt drift
- record prompt/version information internally
- regenerate images that become unusually attractive or dramatic
- manually review every scored image

AI generation does not replace assessment review.

---

## 26. Prohibited Visual Signals

Do not use visual cues that imply:

- correct answer
- best choice
- intelligence
- talent
- popularity
- wealth
- success
- moral superiority
- future prestige

Examples to avoid:

- stars around one activity
- gold glow
- trophy
- teacher approval
- applause
- money
- expensive office
- celebrity-style stage
- “genius” visual symbolism

---

## 27. Professional Review Questions

For every scored illustration ask:

1. Does the image clearly show the intended activity?
2. Could a child choose it mainly because the picture is more exciting?
3. Does the image introduce a second construct?
4. Does it imply wealth or special access?
5. Does it contain gender or cultural stereotypes?
6. Does it depend on physical ability unnecessarily?
7. Does it imply adult approval?
8. Is it comparable in visual energy to other assessment images?
9. Does it match the English wording?
10. Does it match the Chinese wording?

---

## 28. Release Gate

Illustration Concept
→
Content Match Review
→
Attractiveness Balance Review
→
Bias Review
→
Accessibility Review
→
Bilingual Match Review
→
Approved Illustration

No scored illustration enters production solely because it looks visually good.

---

## 29. Visual Principle

The best assessment illustration is not the most beautiful image.

It is the image that helps the child understand the activity while adding the least possible measurement bias.
